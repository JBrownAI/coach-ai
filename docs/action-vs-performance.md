Action Mode vs. Performance Mode

Overview

A central concept in the CoachAI training framework is the distinction between performance mode and action mode during resistance training.

The distinction is particularly relevant when the objective of an exercise is muscular development rather than simply maximizing the amount of external weight moved.

Performance Mode

In performance mode, the primary objective is to accomplish the external task.

The nervous system organizes movement around successfully moving the external resistance from one position to another. The body may utilize multiple muscles, movement strategies, leverage advantages, momentum, and compensations to accomplish the task.

For example, during a lateral raise, the lifter's implicit objective may become:

«"Get the dumbbell higher."»

The resulting movement may accomplish that objective while allowing other structures to contribute substantially to the movement.

Performance mode is not inherently bad. It can be highly appropriate when the objective is performance of the movement itself.

Action Mode

In action mode, the lifter's attention is directed toward the mechanical action of the musculature being trained.

The external resistance becomes the stimulus that makes the desired muscular action more difficult rather than being the primary objective of the movement.

The conceptual sequence is:

Target muscle → desired joint action → movement of the body segment → external resistance is displaced

The goal is not to ignore the external load, but to prevent movement of the external load from becoming the dominant motor objective when the training goal is muscular development.

Example: Dumbbell Lateral Raise

The target musculature includes the lateral deltoid.

The relevant joint action is shoulder abduction.

Performance-oriented execution

The lifter primarily focuses on raising the dumbbell.

Potential consequences include:

- excessive momentum
- excessive torso movement
- altered arm trajectory
- increased contribution from other musculature
- reduced control of the intended movement

Action-oriented execution

The lifter conceptualizes the exercise as raising the arm from the shoulder.

The dumbbell provides resistance to the desired movement rather than becoming the primary focus.

Potential coaching strategies include:

- reducing load when necessary
- increasing control and time under tension
- stabilizing the torso
- minimizing momentum
- focusing attention on the desired movement originating at the shoulder

Example: Bench Press

For hypertrophy-oriented bench pressing, the external goal is moving the bar.

An action-oriented approach instead considers the muscular actions responsible for producing that movement.

The conceptual model is:

Target musculature → shoulder action → humeral movement → force transmission through the arm → bar displacement

This creates a different coaching question.

Instead of asking only:

«"Did the client move the bar?"»

the trainer can ask:

«"Was the movement organized in a way that appropriately challenged the intended musculature?"»

Important Qualification

Action mode should not be interpreted as a claim that external movement is irrelevant or that a single attentional strategy universally produces superior hypertrophy.

The framework is a coaching hypothesis and decision-making model that should be evaluated against biomechanics, motor-control research, hypertrophy research, and practical training outcomes.

The framework should therefore remain open to modification as evidence accumulates.

Potential CoachAI Application

CoachAI could eventually evaluate an exercise using the following sequence:

1. Identify the training objective.
2. Identify the target musculature.
3. Identify the relevant muscular and joint actions.
4. Identify the external movement required by the exercise.
5. Evaluate whether the client's execution prioritizes the intended action.
6. Identify compensatory strategies.
7. Determine whether the resistance is appropriate for maintaining the desired execution.
8. Recommend the smallest intervention necessary.
9. Evaluate the response over subsequent sessions.

This framework is intended to capture expert coaching reasoning rather than simply generate exercise prescriptions.
